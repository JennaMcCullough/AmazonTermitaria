# English version of "POSIBLE USO DE TERMITEROS ARBÓREOS COMO CAVIDADES DE NIDIFICACIÓN POR EL LORO CABEZA AMARILLA _AMAZONA ORATRIX_, UN LORO MESOAMERICANO EN PELIGRO DE EXTINCIÓN"


POTENTIAL USE OF ARBOREAL TERMITARIA AS NESTING CAVITIES BY THE ENDANGERED YELLOW-HEADED AMAZON _AMAZONA ORATRIX_

Jenna McCullough, Victor Gamez, and Raymond VanBuskirk 

**ABSTRACT**

Termite nests are an important nesting substrate for birds, particularly Neotropical parrots. Despite extensive study of the nesting biology of Yellow-headed Amazons (Amazona oratrix), its use of nesting substrates other than tree cavities or nest boxes has not been reported. Here, we report two opportunistic observations of A. oratrix inside cavities of arboreal termitaria in pine savanna habitat during the breeding season in Belize. Our chance observations were made across different years (2021 and 2025) and locations (Orange Walk and Belize districts). These observations suggest that arboreal termitaria may represent an overlooked but rare nesting substrate for this species. We highlight the need for future studies and conservation efforts to consider a broader range of nesting substrates for this endangered parrot species. Furthermore, we encourage land managers to preserve arboreal termitaria where A. oratrix occurs, as these structures may increase the availability of potential nesting where suitable-sized cavities are limited.   

**KEYWORDS**: Arboreal termites, Cavity nesting, Neotropical parrots, Nest-sites, Psittacidae

**INTRODUCTION**

The Yellow-headed Amazon (_Amazona oratrix_) is a large and endangered Mesoamerican parrot (Birdlife International 2020). It is a specialist of tropical lowland deciduous woodlands and pine savannahs ranging from the Pacific and Atlantic slopes of Mexico into Belize, Guatemala, and Honduras (Juniper and Parr 1998). In addition to habitat loss, the species is heavily impacted by the harvesting of eggs and nestlings from wild nests for the illegal pet trade (Berkunsky et al. 2017). Although there are stable, introduced populations in southern Texas and California (Hardy 1973, eBird 2026), the species is scarce across much of its native range (Berlanga et al. 2010).

Due to its endangered status, there has been a considerable effort to document its breeding biology (Enkerlin-Hoeflich 1995, Eisermann 2003, Monterrubio-Rico et al. 2014, Tarazona-Tubens et al. 2020). Breeding pairs lay 2–4 eggs in medium to large secondary cavities, located near the crown of tall trees (Collar 1997, Juniper and Parr 1998), such as in glassywood (_Astronium graveolens_), coma (_Bumelia laetevirens_), Texas ebony (_Ebenopsis ebano_), palo santo (_Bursera arborea_), and pine trees (_Pinus_ sp.; Enkerlin-Hoeflich 1995, Renton 2002, Monterrubio-Rico et al. 2014, Tarazona-Tubens 2022b). Nesting pairs will also occupy artificial nest boxes, often preferring those in taller pines near other previously successful nests (Tarazona-Tubens et al. 2022a, b). In Belize, Tarazona-Tubens (2022b) documented 124 _A. oratrix_ nests over a two-year period: 78 were in live _Pinus_, 38 in artificial nest boxes, and 8 were found in dead trees. 

However, parrots that nest in tree cavities occasionally use alternative substrates, such as termitaria, for nesting (Renton 2015). Proposed explanations for this behavior include limited availability of adequate tree nest cavities, stable nest microclimate, and physical protection or olfactory camouflage from nest predators (Noirot 1970, Brightsmith 2000, Sanchez-Martinez and Renton 2009). Of the 178 species in Psittacidae, 31 have been reported to nest in termitaria (Forshaw 1989, Gill et al. 2025). Documenting variation in nesting substrates contributes to a more complete understanding of avian breeding biology, which is particularly important for species of conservation concern. Here, we provide the first documentation of _A. oratrix_apparently using termitaria as nesting cavities, based on two opportunistic observations in Belize. 

**METHODS**

While recreationally birdwatching in Orange Walk and Belize districts, Belize in 2021 and 2025, we made opportunistic observations with binoculars of three A. oratrix individuals associating with two arboreal termite nests during the breeding season, four years apart. We documented geographic locality, habitat, and behavioral information of these observations with field notes and photographs (a Cannon EOS Rebel T3i with a 150–600 mm lens for observations made in 2021 and a Cannon R6 with 500 mm lens for observations made in 2025). Due to the conservation status of this species and the opportunistic nature in which we made these observations, we only briefly observed (<15 minutes at each site) and photographed birds from afar to limit our impact on the individual birds. Because we did not know the ownership of the land in which the trees were on, we did not approach the termitaria to determine if they were active. 

**RESULTS**

We observed two cases in which adult A. oratrix were associating with arboreal termitaria: one 21 April 2021 approximately 25 km north of Belmopan in Orange Walk District and the other on 29 March 2025 approximately 2 km northwest of the village of Crooked Tree in Belize District (Figure 1). Potential nests were approximately 40 km apart. In both cases, we observed one or two adults near or within an arboreal termitarium located near the crown of large Pinus sp. trees (10–15 m above the ground) in pine savanna habitat. Each termitarium had an excavated entrance slightly larger than the width of the adult bird (Figure 1). During our brief observations, the adults remained silent and were either inside the cavity or perched beside the entrance while their partner was inside. Due to the distance in which we observed the individuals, we could not determine if the termitaria were active colonies.

Our observations of single or paired individuals associating with a termitarium that has an excavated cavity during the breeding season suggests that these two observations are of active nests. These are the first documented cases suggesting that _Amazona oratrix_ could use arboreal termitaria for nesting. Generally, parrots that use alternative nesting substrates like termitaria are smaller bodied (<100 g; Collar 1997, Renton 2015). However, our observations in _A. oratrix_ do not represent the first possible cases of termitaria nesting behavior in the genus. Three Amazona species—Turquoise-fronted (_A. aestiva_, 400 g), Yellow-crowned (_A. ochrocephala_, 340–535 g), and Red-tailed (_A. brasiliensis_) Amazons—have been documented to nest in either ground or arboreal termitaria, and in these species, it is considered rare (Forshaw 1989, Martuscelli 1995, Collar 1997, Collar et al. 2020a, b). In Belize, Tarazona-Tubens (2022b) did not record any nests of _A. oratrix_ in termitaria among 124 monitored nests. But termitaria were not explicitly targeted for surveys. In that study, the main cause of nest failure was predation by snakes, iguanas, and other bird species (woodpeckers, falcons, hawks). Bird nests within arboreal termitaria often also contain active termite colonies that could provide a degree of physical protection or mask the scent from potential nest predators (Hardy 1963, Brightsmith 2000, Sanchez-Martinez and Renton 2009). However, we were unable to confirm termite activity in the nests observed here.

An alternative explanation for the behaviors we observed could be that individuals were exploiting arboreal termite nests as a food resource. All published dietary records of _A. oratrix_ are restricted to plant material (Enkerlin-Hoeflich 1995, Collar 1997, Juniper and Parr 1998, Renton 2002). Termite consumption has not, to our knowledge, been reported for _A. oratrix_ or other Amazona species (Collar 1997). Overall, neotropical parrots are rarely recorded feeding on insects. For example, in a two-year study documenting 1,469 feeding observations across 16 wild parrot species in the western Amazon, only three observations involved termite consumption by two parakeet species (Lee et al. 2014). Both species are smaller-bodied and differ in their foraging ecologies compared to other larger-bodied parrots in that study. Records of termite-eating in parrots include aerial foraging, direct bark excavations, and destruction of the outer layer of the termitarium (Sazima 1989, Sick 1997, Faria 2007, de Paula et al. 2017). For example, Peach-fronted Parakeets (Eupsittula aurea, 74–94 g) will actively excavate the surface of the termitarium, producing numerous small holes through repeated bill-probing (de Faria 2007). In contrast, our observations from a distance and photographs did not show either termitarium to have multiple smaller holes consistent with termite excavation for food (Figure 1). Instead, each contained a single large cavity consistent with a potential nest entrance. Because we did not directly view offspring or adults carrying food to the termitaria, we cannot exclude opportunistic foraging. But the lack of excavation behavior and the presence of a single large, excavated hole are more consistent with cavity inspection and use for nesting than termite-feeding behavior. 

Our observations reported here constitute the first known cases of _A. oratrix_ using arboreal termitaria and highlights that this species may occasionally exploit this nesting substrate. Future studies of nesting ecology of this endangered species should explicitly consider arboreal termitaria for potential nest sites, which may represent an overlooked but potentially important nesting resource. Moreover, we urge land managers to preserve arboreal termitaria as a potential nesting resource where this species occurs, especially in cases where adequate natural cavities are limited.
