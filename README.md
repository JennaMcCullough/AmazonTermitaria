# English version of "Posible uso de termiteros arbóreos como cavidades de nidificación por parte de los loro cabeza amarilla _Amazona oratrix_, un loro mesoamericano en peligro de extinción"


LIKELY USE OF ARBOREAL TERMITARIA AS NESTING CAVITIES BY YELLOW-HEADED AMAZON AMAZONA ORATRIX, AN ENDANGERED MESOAMERICAN PARROT 

Jenna McCullough, Victor Gamez, and Raymond VanBuskirk 

**ABSTRACT**

Termite nests are an important nesting substrate for birds, particularly Neotropical parrots. Despite extensive study of the nesting biology of Yellow-headed Amazons (_Amazona oratrix_), its use of nesting substrates other than tree cavities or nest boxes has not been reported. Here, we describe two opportunistic observations of _A. oratrix_ inside cavities of arboreal termitaria in pine savanna habitat during the breeding season in Belize. These observations suggest that arboreal termitaria may represent an overlooked but rare nesting substrate for this species. We highlight the need for future studies and conservation efforts to consider a broader range of nesting substrates for this endangered parrot species. Moreover, we encourage land managers to preserve arboreal termitaria where _A. oratrix_ occurs to increase potential nest sites if sufficiently large cavities are limited.  

**KEYWORDS**: Arboreal termites, Cavity nesting, Illegal pet trade, Neotropics Nesting biology, Psittacidae 

The Yellow-headed Amazon _Amazona oratrix_ Ridgway, 1887 is a large and endangered Mesoamerican parrot. It is a specialist of tropical lowland deciduous woodlands and pine savannahs ranging from the Pacific and Atlantic slopes of Mexico into Belize, Guatemala, and Honduras (Juniper and Parr 1998). In addition to habitat loss, the species is heavily impacted by the harvesting of eggs and nestlings from wild nests for the illegal pet trade. Although there are stable, introduced populations in southern Texas and California (Hardy 1973), eBird 2026), the species is scarce across much of its native range (Berlanga et al. 2010). 

Due to its endangered status, there has been a considerable effort to document its nesting biology (Enkerlin-Hoeflich 1995, Eisermann 2003, Tarazona-Tubens et al. 2022b). Breeding pairs lay 2–4 eggs in medium to large secondary cavities near the crown of tall trees (Juniper and Parr 1998). The species has a narrow range of preferred cavity trees, such as glassywood (Astronium graveolens), palo santo (Bursera arborea), and pine trees (_Pinus_ sp., (Renton 2002). Nesting pairs will also occupy artificial nest boxes, often preferring those in taller pines near other previously successful nests (Tarazona-Tubens et al. 2022a, b). In Belize, Tarazona-Tubens (2022b) documented 124 _A. oratrix_ nests over a two-year period, of which 78 were in live _Pinus_, 38 in artificial nest boxes, and 8 were found in dead trees. Despite this effort, the full range of nesting substrates used by this species remains incompletely characterized.

Here, we report the first known cases of likely nest use of _A. oratrix_ in arboreal termitaria. Both observations were made opportunistically while birdwatching during the breeding season in Belize: one in April 2021 approximately 25 km north of Belmopan in Orange Walk District and the other in March 2025 approximately 2 km northwest of the village of Crooked Tree in Belize District (Figure 1). In both cases, we observed one or two adults near or within an arboreal termitarium located near the crown of large Pinus sp trees (10–15 m above the ground) in pine savanna habitat. Each termitarium had a cavity entrance slightly larger than the width of the adult bird. During our brief observations, the adults remained silent and were either inside the cavity or perched beside the entrance while their partner was inside. Given that these observations occurred during the breeding season in preferred breeding habitat (Juniper and Parr 1998), we infer that these structures were likely being used as nest sites.

An alternative explanation for this behavior could be that individuals were exploiting arboreal termite nests as a food resource. All published diet records of _A. oratrix_ are restricted to plant material (Enkerlin-Hoeflich 1995, Juniper and Parr 1998), Renton 2002). Termite consumption has not, to our knowledge, been reported for _A. oratrix_ or other Amazona species. Moreover, neotropical parrots are rarely recorded feeding on insects. In a two-year study documenting 1,469 feeding observations across 16 wild parrot species in the western Amazon, only three observations involved termite consumption: one by Dusky-headed Parakeets (_Aratinga weddellii_; one of 15 total feeding observations) and two by Cobalt-winged Parakeets (_Brotogeris cyanoptera_; two of 254 feeding observations; (Lee et al. 2014). Both species are smaller-bodied and differ in their foraging ecologies compared to other larger bodied parrots, including two _Amazona_ species, in Lee et al. (2014). Similarly, Peach-fronted Parakeets (_Eupsittula aurea_) have been reported feeding on termites (Sazima 1989, de Faria 2007), but the behavior differed markedly from our observations. Individuals actively excavated the outer surface of the termitarium, producing numerous small holes through repeated bill-probing (de Faria 2007). In contrast, we observed no evidence of active excavation at either termitarium. Instead, each contained a single large cavity consistent with a potential nest entrance. Though we cannot exclude opportunistic foraging, the lack of excavation behavior and the presence of a single large hole are more consistent with cavity inspection and potential nesting than termite-feeding behavior. 

Parrots will use arboreal termitaria for nesting, which may reduce predation risk and maintain optimal microclimate conditions (Noirot 1970, Brightsmith 2000, Sanchez-martinez and Renton 2009). Of the 31 parrot species reported to nest in termitaria, only two species in the genus _Amazona_—Turquoise-fronted (_A. aestiva_) and Red-tailed (_A. brasiliensis_) Amazons—have been documented to do so, and in these species it is considered rare (Forshaw 1989, Martuscelli 1995, Collar et al. 2020). In Belize, Tarazona-Tubens (2022b) did not record any nests of _A. oratrix_ in termitaria among 124 monitored nests. But termitaria were not explicitly targeted for surveys. In that study, the main cause of nest failure was predation by snakes, iguanas, and other bird species (woodpeckers, falcons, hawks). Bird nests within arboreal termitaria often also contain active termite colonies that could provide a degree of physical protection or mask the scent from potential nest predators (Hardy 1963, Brightsmith 2000, Sanchez-martinez and Renton 2009). However, we were unable to confirm termite activity in the nests observed here. 

These chance observations constitute the first known cases of _A. oratrix_ likely using arboreal termitaria and suggest that this species may occasionally exploit this nesting substrate. Given the  opportunistic nature of our observations, we could not determine the stage of the nesting cycle in either case. Future studies of nesting ecology of this endangered species should explicitly consider arboreal termitaria for potential nest sites, which may represent an overlooked but potentially important nesting resource. Moreover, we urge land managers to preserve arboreal termitaria as a potential nesting resource where this species occurs, especially in cases where adequate natural cavities are limited.
